## What Went Wrong/Was Hard

Several challenges came from the data rather than the algorithms themselves. One of the most time-consuming issues was NaN in the forward pass. Missing values in the TCGA methylation data weren’t immediately obvious, and the model would fail silently by producing NaN log-likelihoods. I fixed it using the mean per site for each NaN value, but diagnosing it took me a while. Another major issue was CpG site ordering. The HMM assumes that consecutive sites are genomic neighbors, but initially the sites were ordered by variance instead of chromosomal position. This broke the sequential assumption and led to unrealistic results. Sorting by genomic position improved the behavior, but the selected high-variance sites may not form contiguous genomic regions, which still limits the HMM. The mutation data also introduced complications. The MAF file was much larger than expected, with over 11,000 unique genes across 461 patients. Filtering to recurrent genes helped, but the resulting domain features remained sparse and low in variance. Additionally, patient barcode mismatches across datasets caused an unexpected issue. IDs appeared in multiple formats (12-character vs. 15-character), initially resulting in zero overlap between datasets. Standardizing IDs to a consistent format resolved the problem.

My personal journey with this project was full of road bumps and obstacles. I had to spend a significant amount of time learning so much about the different algorithms and ML concepts, which made the process quite tiring at times. Breaking down the complexity and making sense of what to do, how to do it, and why I am doing it was a huge hurdle to overcome, and I found myself pushed more as a programmer than a biologist. ## Algorithmic Lessons

One key takeaway was the importance of the sequential order for the HMM. The HMM only produces meaningful results when the data respects genomic ordering. When that assumption was violated, the model still ran, but the results were biologically meaningless. This reinforced how critical preprocessing is, sometimes even more so than the algorithm itself. Another was that Baum–Welch is highly sensitive to initialization. Even though it eventually reaches convergence, it only finds a local optimum. Using biologically informed starting values with means near 0.1 and 0.8 led to meaningful states, whereas random initialization would likely have produced unreliable solutions. Another limitation came from feature sparsity in the mutation data. Profile HMMs were really neat to learn about and work with, but their effectiveness depends on having enough signal. With only a handful of mutations per patient and thousands of possible domains, my data was not the highest standard for this algorithm. Despite this, even a small number of regulatory features made a measurable difference. Adding just three HMM-derived features (fraction active, fraction repressed, and number of transitions) to 500 RNA features led to a consistent improvement in macro F1 score. While the gain was modest, it suggests that epigenetic information provides a slightly better signal beyond gene expression alone.

Moreover, from the workflow and its results, I was able to make some interesting findings about the data. RNA alone serves as a strong baseline, achieving high performance (macro F1 = 0.765), which was already expected. Adding methylation-based regulatory features from the HMM provides a small improvement of +0.003. In contrast, domain mutation features do not improve performance and instead slightly reduce the macro F1 by -0.005. Overall, these results show that multi-omics integration is not guaranteed to improve prediction. Although the full model performs best, the gains are small and not significantly different. In conclusion, regulatory features derived from methylation provide a slight improvement over RNA alone, while mutation-based domain features do not. These results should be interpreted with caution. They are based on a single random seed and include a small number of samples for some subtypes, which is not the most robust and thorough scientific approach.

## Future Directions

There are several clear directions for improving this work. One would be to incorporate genomic context more explicitly, for example, by weighting CpG sites based on their proximity to promoters or enhancers, since promoter methylation is more likely to affect gene expression. Another extension would be to move from a two-state to a three-state HMM, allowing for an intermediate methylation state. This seems especially relevant given that the learned “repressed” state fell in an intermediate range rather than near the expected high-methylation level. On the mutation side, aggregating features at the pathway level rather than the individual gene or domain level could help address sparsity and better capture cooperative effects between mutations. Additionally, with more computational resources, training the HMM on the full cohort rather than a subset of 200 patients would likely produce more stable parameter estimates. Finally, extending the analysis to include survival prediction would provide a more direct test of clinical relevance, going beyond subtype classification to evaluate whether multi-omics features can improve outcome prediction.
